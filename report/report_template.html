1Executive summary

In 2021 I designed a service-management database for an auto repair shop as my term project for IS 475, Database Design and Implementation, at UNLV. In 2026 I audited it, found that it no longer ran and that several of its answers were wrong, and rebuilt it end to end: schema, test data, reporting layer, 22 analysis queries, data-quality tests and an interactive dashboard.

The problem

The original report named two root problems: (1) jobs and appointments running late, which drives customers away, and (2) no tracking of parts usage, which leads to stockouts. The 2021 design couldn't measure either: it linked parts to mechanics instead of jobs, and each mechanic to a single customer.

Top recommendations

  1. Reset reorder points on 11 parts using lead-time demand plus safety stock. It costs about $3,570 in extra stock, 21% more than today's $16.6K shelf.
  2. Build supplier ETAs into promise times and hold the electrical supplier to its quoted lead time (it delivers late 94% of the time).
  3. Send reminders for appointments booked 8+ days out, and cap the 8:00 AM slot at two check-ins per service writer.
  4. Add the comeback rate to technician reviews, and keep pairing apprentices with master technicians (the first apprentice's efficiency rose from 75% to 90%).

2Business problem

The business is an independent, general-repair auto shop in the southwest Las Vegas valley with 6 technicians, about 4,000 repair orders a year and roughly $1.4 million in annual sales.

The 2021 report described a shop in trouble: appointments were delayed or double-booked, mechanics couldn't work efficiently because parts were out of stock, nobody recorded which parts were used, and the resulting delays were costing customers and revenue. The shop had no database, so it couldn't study any of these patterns.

That diagnosis still holds, but the 2021 database couldn't confirm it. Answering "are late jobs costing us customers?" requires linking an appointment to a job, the job to the parts it used and the technician who did it, and the customer to their next visit. Version 2 is designed around exactly those links.

4Business rules

Each rule maps to a constraint in sql/01_schema.sql or a test in sql/05_data_quality_checks.sql, so the rules are enforced, not just documented.

BR-1A customer owns one or more vehicles; each vehicle belongs to exactly one customer. A VIN is unique and valid.FK, UNIQUE, CHECK (VIN pattern); test 1, 3
BR-2An appointment is for one vehicle at one time. It is Scheduled, Arrived, No-Show or Cancelled.FK, CHECK; tests 5, 6

BR-7A warranty comeback points to the original job, is on the same vehicle, happens after it, and is never billed.Self-referencing FK; tests 11, 12
BR-8Every part that enters stock is recorded as an opening balance, a replenishment, or a special order for one job. Stock can never go negative.CHECK; tests 18, 20

6Normalization & design decisions

First normal form. Every column holds one value. The 2021 Customer_Name became first_name and last_name, and Appt_Date plus a text Appt_Time ('12:21 PM') became one DATETIME.

Second normal form. The two junction tables (work_order_service and work_order_part) have composite keys, and every other column depends on the whole key. Hours billed depend on this service on this job, not on the service alone.

Third normal form. No column depends on another non-key column:

  • No stored totals. Invoice totals, stock on hand and gross profit are calculated in views (v_invoice_totals, v_part_inventory, v_work_order_summary). A stored total can drift from its line items; a derived one can't.
  • Customer reached through the vehicle. appointment and work_order don't store customer_id, because the vehicle already determines the customer (a transitive dependency).

Deliberate trade-offs

  • Prices on transaction lines. work_order_part.unit_price repeats the catalog price, but it's a fact about the sale: when the catalog price changes, old invoices must not change. Maintenance example 6 demonstrates this.
  • work_order.vehicle_id alongside appointment_id. About a third of jobs are walk-ins with no appointment, so the job needs its own vehicle link. Data-quality test 4 guarantees the two never disagree.
  • Surrogate integer keys everywhere, with natural keys (VIN, SKU, email) kept UNIQUE. The 2021 text keys such as 'C-42 ' (trailing space included) are gone.
  • Current wage for labor cost. Labor cost uses each technician's current wage. A wage-history table would be the next step for exact historical cost.

Reporting layer

Seven views turn the normalized tables into analysis-ready facts. The dashboard, the Power BI/Tableau exports and the analysis queries all read from these views, so every tool uses one definition of every metric:

v_invoice_totalsOne row per invoice: labor, parts, discount, tax, total, paid, balance due.
v_appointment_summaryOne row per appointment: lead time, slot, outcome, check-in wait, bookings in the same slot.

7Data, simulation & quality testing

The 2021 data had 50 hand-typed rows per table (the course minimum), every relationship 1:1. That's too small to analyze and too simple to expose bugs like double counting. Version 2 is loaded with about 70,000 rows from a seeded simulation of two years of shop operations.

How the data was generated

python/generate_data.py (standard library only, seed 2021) simulates the shop one business day at a time:

  • New and returning customers arrive. Returning customers come back roughly every four months, and are less likely to return if their last job was late or poorly rated.
  • Customers book appointments (lead time 0–30 days) or walk in. Long-lead bookings no-show more often; the 7:30 and 8:00 AM slots fill first.
  • Each visit gets 1–3 services. Seasonal demand follows Las Vegas conditions: A/C work peaks at 2–3× in summer, batteries and cooling systems about 2×.
  • Each technician works one job at a time with a personal speed (efficiency) and comeback rate. Apprentices improve over time.
  • Parts come off a simulated shelf with reorder points and supplier lead times. An empty shelf triggers a special order and a parts wait.
  • Completed jobs are invoiced (Nevada taxes parts, not labor), paid (fleet accounts on 30-day terms) and sometimes rated.
The patterns in Section 8 were designed into the simulation, for example under-stocked brake rotors and one technician with a high comeback rate. The point of the project is not to discover them; it's to show that the database, queries and dashboard can surface and quantify problems like these from raw transactions, which is the job a data analyst does with real data.

Data-quality test suite

Constraints block most bad data at insert time. sql/05_data_quality_checks.sql covers the rules that span rows and tables. It runs 20 checks and returns one PASS/FAIL row per check. All 20 pass.

#CheckTechnique
3Every VIN's check digit (position 9) is validRecursive CTE over 17 positions, transliteration, weighted MOD 11
9Odometer never goes backwards for a vehicleLAG() over each vehicle's visits
18Every out-of-stock part line has a matching special order, and vice versaCorrelated EXISTS
20Stock on hand never goes negativeRunning SUM() OVER across a receipts/usage ledger
1–19Plus orphan, duplicate, date-order, invoice, payment and status-consistency checksAnti-joins, GROUP BY … HAVING

The suite caught real bugs. On its first run, 3 checks failed. One was a genuine error in my generator: a returning customer could book their next visit before their previous visit had happened. The other two were edge cases in the checks themselves: an appointment at exactly the snapshot time, and two cars finished at 11:30 AM on the snapshot day and not yet picked up. I fixed the generator, tightened the two checks, and documented each case.

Each finding below names the query that produced it (sql/04_analysis_queries.sql). Charts come from the operations dashboard with the full 24 months selected.

Growth came from a higher average ticket, helped by a labor-rate increase from $140 to $150 per hour, while car count was nearly flat. Sales peak every summer (June–August 2025 averaged $150K a month vs. $116K the rest of the year) because Las Vegas heat drives A/C, battery and cooling repairs (Q15: A/C work runs at 2.5× its average month in July and 0.15× in December). Brakes are the largest category at 18% of sales and the most profitable overall (Q3).

Q4 is the corrected version of 2021 query 11. European makes (BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Volkswagen, Audi) average 7–17% above the shop's typical ticket because their flat-rate labor times are longer.

8.2 Why jobs run late: parts, not people

Only about 76% of jobs finish by the time promised at drop-off. Q5 splits on-time performance by every factor the database records:

Parts availability dominates. When every part was on the shelf, 79.6% of jobs finished on time. When a normally stocked part had run out, only 11.0% did, and turnaround jumped from 6 hours to 101. Technician skill matters much less (apprentices 70%, master technicians 82%). Summer's lower on-time rate (73.5%) is partly summer stockouts and partly workload: even with every part on the shelf, summer jobs were on time 77.5% of the time vs. 80.4% the rest of the year. Walk-ins and appointments perform about the same (75% vs. 77%).

Parts delays touched only about 5% of jobs but account for 17% of all late jobs. They're the most concentrated, and the most fixable, cause of the shop's core 2021 problem.

8.3 Parts stockouts: the reorder points are simply too low

Stockouts cluster in two places: front brake rotors, the shop's highest-volume repair part, and summer parts (AGM batteries, A/C compressors, leak-detection dye) whose demand doubles in the heat while reorder points stay the same. Q12 compares each part's reorder point with a recommended one calculated in v_part_inventory:

recommended reorder point = avg daily use × avg actual lead time
                          + 1.65 × σ(daily use) × √(lead time)      -- 95% service level

Front rotors need a reorder point of 10, not 2; the Group H6 battery 7, not 2; alternators 4, not 1. Raising all 11 affected parts costs about $3,570 in extra stock, set against 229 jobs over two years that waited an average of 4.1 days for parts and rated the shop 2.5 out of 5.

Suppliers make it worse (Q13). Summit Electrical Components quotes 3 days and averages 4.9; 94% of its orders arrive late. Because the reorder-point formula uses actual lead times, it already compensates, but the shop should hold Summit to its quote or move volume to another supplier.

8.4 Appointments: long-lead bookings no-show, and 8 AM is overbooked

No-shows rise with booking lead time (Q7): 3.0% for appointments made 0–2 days out, 6.6% at 3–7 days, 13.9% at 8–14 days and 19.7% at 15+ days. A reminder text for anything booked a week or more ahead targets the 21% of bookings that produce nearly half (48%) of all no-shows.

The 8:00 AM slot is the 2021 "clashing appointments" problem (Q6). It averages 2.5 bookings per slot, 45% of its customers arrive into an overbooked slot, and 15% wait more than 15 minutes to be checked in, vs. under 2% after noon. With two service writers, the fix is to cap each half-hour slot at two check-ins and move the overflow to 8:30–9:30.

8.5 Technicians: one quality outlier, and a working apprentice program

Efficiency (flat-rate hours billed ÷ hours clocked) ranges from 114% for the senior master technician to 73% for the newest apprentice (Q9). Quality is a separate question. Tyler Brooks was an average-speed technician (91%) but had a 7.7% comeback rate, 2.6× everyone else. His 70 comebacks cost $5,046 in unbilled rework (Q11, a self-join from each rework to its original job), and customers rated those original jobs 2.8 out of 5. He left in May 2025.

The apprentice program works (Q10). Kevin Tran's efficiency rose every quarter, from 74.6% in Q1 2024 to 89.5% in Q4 2025 (a LAG() window over quarterly results). Jordan Kim, hired in June 2025, is on the same curve (69% → 79% in two quarters).

8.6 Customers: a late first visit costs a third of repeat business

This is the central finding (Q16). Among new retail customers who had six months to come back, 47.9% returned after an on-time first visit but only 30.8% after a late one. It uses ROW_NUMBER() to find each customer's first visit and LEAD() to find their next one. Applied to the 445 customers whose first job was late, the gap is about 76 lost returning customers, or roughly $29,000 in next visits at the 2025 average repair order of $387, before counting lifetime value. Ratings tell the same story (Q20): 4.47 when on time, 3.21 when late, and 2.75 after waiting on an ordered part, with 43% of those reviews at 1–2 stars.

Referrals bring the best retail customers (Q18): a 56.6% repeat rate and $798 in sales per customer, vs. 38.1% and $573 from social media. Fleet accounts are the most valuable of all ($1,477 per account, 81% repeat). The cohort view (Q17) shows about a third of each quarter's new customers returning the following quarter, and about 10–16% still visiting a year later. RFM segmentation (Q19, NTILE(5)) finds 17% of customers ("Champions") produce 36% of sales, and 430 "at risk" high-value customers average 17 months since their last visit, a ready-made win-back list.

Finance (Q21–Q22). Mobile-wallet payments grew from 7.6% to 12.8% of transactions while cash fell from 11.7% to 8.4%. Accounts receivable is small and current: $13,260 owed by fleet accounts, 85% of it under 30 days old.

11Limitations & next steps

  • Synthetic data. The patterns are realistic but were designed into the simulation. The same schema, queries and dashboard would run unchanged on a real shop's data.
  • Simplifications. One location, current wages for labor cost, and no per-line time tracking (clocked hours are recorded per service, but technicians aren't split within a job).
  • Next steps. Add wage history and a promise-time audit trail; schedule a nightly refresh from the shop system (Python + cron, or dbt on a cloud warehouse); forecast weekly parts demand to set seasonal reorder points automatically; build a churn model that predicts which customers won't return.
